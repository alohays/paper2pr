## Executive Verdict

The speaker notes are already strongest when Yunsung sounds like an operator who has shipped real things, made mistakes, and is trying to save students time. Those notes feel credible, warm, and memorable.

The weak notes fall into three recurring traps:

1. **Textbook voice**: framework definitions, taxonomy walks, or lecture-style explanations.
2. **Generic career-advice voice**: useful content, but interchangeable with any LinkedIn post or career-center workshop.
3. **Formality drift**: especially on Slides 51-62, where contractions disappear and the speaker suddenly sounds scripted.

## Biggest Tone Problems

1. **Slides 51-62 have a clear formality cliff.** The mentor voice turns into "do not / it is / you are" prose. Content is still solid, but the delivery will sound colder and less human.
2. **Several mid-deck advice slides narrate frameworks instead of coaching decisions.** Slides 5, 11, 21, 25, 30, 31, 41, 47, and 50 are the clearest examples.
3. **The best notes are concrete and personal. The weaker ones become abstract.** Whenever the notes move from "here is what happened to me" to "here is a classification system," the personality drops.

## Weakest 10 Notes and Suggested Rewrites

These are the ten notes I would rewrite first if the goal is to make the whole talk sound like one consistent person: experienced, blunt, warm, and credible.

### 1. Slide 3 — The Wave Is Here
**Why it misses:** Too much roadmap narration. Not enough threat, promise, or voice.

**Rewrite:**
> This wave is not waiting for your graduation ceremony. It is already here, and it is already rearranging who gets hired, what gets paid, and what skills still matter. Tonight I want to do three things for you: show you the wave, show you the two waves I caught, and show you how to build the surfboard you’ll need before the market makes that decision for you.

### 2. Slide 5 — The AI Job Map
**Why it misses:** Feels like walking around a diagram with a laser pointer.

**Rewrite:**
> When students say, "I want to work in AI," my first reaction is: doing what exactly? AI is not one job. Some people push the frontier, some build product, some turn models into business decisions, and some turn messy data into leverage. If you do not know which lane you want, the market will happily choose for you.

### 3. Slide 11 — From "Vibe Coding" to "Agentic Engineering"
**Why it misses:** Too much terminology, not enough plain-English interpretation.

**Rewrite:**
> Last year AI felt like a very smart autocomplete. This year it feels more like a junior team you have to manage well. That is the real shift. The valuable person is no longer the one who can type the most code by hand. It is the one who can break a problem apart, aim the agents, catch bad judgment, and still own the result.

### 4. Slide 21 — Skills That Actually Mattered
**Why it misses:** Sounds like a workshop slide summary instead of lived advice.

**Rewrite:**
> At Wrtn, nobody cared that I knew PyTorch. They cared whether I could figure out what the real problem was, move fast without getting sloppy, and stay close enough to users to notice when we were being clever in the wrong direction. Those are not glamorous skills. They are just the skills that make you useful.

### 5. Slide 25 — Maum.ai WoRV
**Why it misses:** Corporate overview tone. Too abstract for a personal story pivot.

**Rewrite:**
> My job now is simple to say and hard to do: make robots work outside PowerPoint. At WoRV, that means three things have to connect in the real world, not just in a research demo: navigation, manipulation, and simulation. If even one of those is weak, the robot looks impressive in a lab and embarrassing in production.

### 6. Slide 30 — Open Source Contribution
**Why it misses:** Opens with stats when the more human career lesson is stronger.

**Rewrite:**
> A lot of students think open source is a side quest. It is not. Open source is one of the clearest ways to show how you think, how you work, and whether you can build with other people in public. The project numbers matter because they prove scale, but the real career value is simpler: your GitHub can start speaking for you before you enter the room.

### 7. Slide 31 — Infrastructure = Research
**Why it misses:** Sounds like a hardware briefing, not career advice.

**Rewrite:**
> Let me give you one very unsexy truth about research: a lot of "research speed" is actually infrastructure speed. Before CORE, good ideas waited around for slow machines and slow storage. After CORE, the exact same researchers could move like a different team. So when you join a lab or company, do not just ask what they are working on. Ask what kind of engine they are trying to work with.

### 8. Slide 41 — What to Build This Month
**Why it misses:** Too many choices, too many tool names, not enough decisive coaching.

**Rewrite:**
> If you are stuck on what to build, stop looking for a genius idea. Pick something annoying, visible, and demoable. Build one thing that saves time, one thing that uses AI in a real workflow, or one thing that makes somebody say, "Oh, I would actually use that." The goal is not originality on day one. The goal is shipped evidence.

### 9. Slide 47 — Technical Interview Prep
**Why it misses:** Framework-first, emotionally flat, and too classroom-like.

**Rewrite:**
> Technical interviews are not one test. They are four trapdoors stacked on top of each other. If your fundamentals are shaky, you never get credit for system design. If your system thinking is weak, nobody cares that you memorized transformer equations. So the better question is not, "Did I study?" It is, "Which layer would reject me today?"

### 10. Slide 50 — Year-by-Year Roadmap
**Why it misses:** Useful, but sounds generic and evenly narrated.

**Rewrite:**
> Do not memorize this whole roadmap. Just know your year and your job. First year: explore and get curious. Second year: build. Third year: ship something real and public. Fourth year: turn that proof into internships, interviews, and offers. You do not need all four steps today. You just need to stop drifting on the one you are in.

## Priority Fix Order

If time is limited, I would fix the notes in this order:

1. Slides 51-62 contraction/formality pass
2. Slides 3, 11, 25, 31, 47
3. Slides 5, 21, 30, 41, 50

## Bottom Line

This deck already has a strong speaker inside it. The best notes sound like someone who has actually built, failed, learned, and returned to hand students a shorter path.

The revision goal is not to make the notes more polished. It is to make them more unmistakably human. Whenever a note starts sounding like an explainer article, a framework handout, or a corporate summary, it should be pulled back toward voice, stakes, and judgment.
